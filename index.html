About

My journey into AI started with a PC, a curious six-year-old, and a lot of questions.

That curiosity took me from programming and algorithms to AI, machine learning, computer vision, and LLMs. Along the way, I’ve worked on everything from predicting air quality from sky images to making AI systems more efficient and scalable.

Today, I’m an AI/ML Engineer & Researcher driven by the same question that started it all: How can we make technology work better?

I was six when I started sitting beside my brother while he configured PCs. I barely understood what he was doing, but I was fascinated by the idea that a machine could be taken apart, rebuilt, and made to do things you imagined.

By the end of elementary school, I was programming in Pascal and learning about algorithms and flowcharts. In high school, I moved to C++, and I quickly discovered that I loved solving problems, especially the kind that don’t have an obvious answer.

That mindset eventually took me to the Computer Olympiad, where I represented my province. I enjoyed the challenge of sitting with a difficult problem until I could finally break it down.

At university, I studied Computer Science and became interested in AI, machine learning, and algorithms. My interest in genetic algorithms led me to develop an approach that combined them with a belief space to improve their performance.

During my master’s, I explored NLP, computer vision, and neuroscience. I worked on a vision model inspired by the human visual system for online signature recognition, and later began a long-term project on air-quality prediction from sky images using image processing and neural networks.

That project taught me something I still value in research: sometimes the interesting problems are the ones where the answer isn’t directly visible. With air pollution, we had to learn from subtle visual effects while accounting for many other environmental factors.

My research eventually led to being ranked first in Computer Science and across the School of Science.

During my PhD, I returned to a question that had been following me since those early days: efficiency. This time, I was looking at it through the lens of LLMs and AI systems. My work focuses on software-hardware co-design to make AI more energy-efficient and scalable.

technology has changed dramatically since I was six. My curiosity hasn’t.

Outside of research, I’m usually looking for something new traveling, trying unfamiliar food, or exploring anything that catches my attention.

At the end of the day, I’m still driven by the same instinct:

If I don’t understand how something works, I want to figure it out.
